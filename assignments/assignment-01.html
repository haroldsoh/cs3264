Stump the Model

Course: CS3264 Foundations of Machine Learning Individual work Deadline: Friday 13 November 2026, 23:59 (last Friday of lectures)

In one sentence: write six math questions that a specific AI model gets wrong, and explain why you think it will get them wrong.

This is not about writing hard or tricky questions for its own sake. It is about finding a real weak spot in how a model reasons — the kind of weak spot you can only find if you actually understand the material yourself. Simply asking an AI tool to "make a hard question" will not work reliably, because you also need to know the model well enough to predict, in advance, why it will fail. That is the actual skill being tested here.

1

What you need to do

Write 6 original questions. Each question must:

  • Be about a topic from this course (see the list of topics in Section 2)
  • Have exactly one correct number as the answer — no multiple parts, no open-ended discussion
  • Be solvable with pen and paper — no figures, no data files, no code needed to solve it
  • Come with your own full written solution (we call this the derivation)
  • Come with a short guess, in your own words, about why the AI model will get it wrong

Across your 6 questions, you must use at least 4 different topics from the list in Section 2, and no more than 2 questions on the same topic. This stops everyone from writing all 6 questions about the one topic that happens to be easiest to break.

A full example

Here is one real, complete question, written the way yours should look:

Topic: kernels_svm  ·  Reason: definition_ambiguity

Question: A hard-margin SVM has weight vector w = [6, 8]. The two supporting hyperplanes are w·x + b = 1 and w·x + b = −1. Report the perpendicular distance between these two hyperplanes.

Your solution: ‖w‖ = √(6² + 8²) = √100 = 10. The full margin width is 2/‖w‖ = 2/10 = 0.2.

Answer: 0.2   (accept anything within 1% of this)

Why you think the model will fail: Models sometimes report 1/‖w‖ instead — the distance from just one hyperplane to the decision boundary — because the word "margin" is used loosely in different textbooks to mean either quantity. The full distance between the two hyperplanes is 2/‖w‖, not 1/‖w‖.

This question is well formed: it is short, self-contained, has exactly one number as the answer, and the written reason is specific to this exact question rather than a generic guess.

Note

This example is here to show the format. It is not an actual stump: Qwen3-1.7B answers it correctly with 0.2. It is also the sample question in the notebook, so you can see this for yourself. A well-formed question with a plausible failure reason is the starting point; only running it against the model, as in Section 5, tells you whether the model actually falls for it.

5

The model you are trying to beat

We use one fixed AI model for the whole term, named exactly below. It is picked so it is neither too weak (everyone would beat it easily) nor too strong (nobody could beat it) — it sits in a range where a genuinely good question can succeed and a weak one cannot.

Main model

Qwen3-1.7B (Hugging Face id: Qwen/Qwen3-1.7B), run with these fixed settings:

  • Greedy decoding — the model always picks its single most likely next word, no randomness
  • Reasoning ("thinking") turned on — the model writes out its working before the final answer
  • Up to 8192 tokens of output per question, so the model has room to finish its reasoning
  • A fixed system prompt, plus one fixed instruction appended to every question asking for a final numeric answer on its own line — both are printed in full in the notebook

Validate your question with the provided notebook

A ready-to-run notebook, cs3264_assignment1_test_your_question.ipynb, is provided together with this document. Open it directly in Google Colab (free, needs only a Google account), go to Runtime → Change runtime type and select a T4 GPU, then paste in your question text, your final answer, and your tolerance. It downloads and runs the exact same model with the exact same settings used for grading — greedy decoding, thinking mode on, the same system prompt and the same fixed instruction appended to your question — and tells you directly whether the model matches your answer.

The notebook also runs your question a second time and checks the two runs agree. Because decoding is greedy, this confirms the result is deterministic on that machine. Grading is run on the same type of GPU as Colab's free tier (an NVIDIA T4) with the same software settings, so the notebook is the closest preview of grading you can get.

The notebook reports one of four verdicts: NOT STUMPED, STUMPED, STUMPED (no readable number), or NO RESULT. The last one means the model ran out of its 8192-token budget before finishing, usually because it fell into a repetition loop. Such a run earns no stump marks in grading (see Section 6), so if you see it, rewrite the question until the model commits to a definite answer.

A quick, informal check: Qwen Chat

You can also get a rough first impression of how today's AI models handle your question on Qwen Chat. This is faster, but less reliable: Qwen Chat currently only offers newer Qwen versions than the one used for grading, and it does not let you control decoding settings, so it cannot confirm reproducibility the way the notebook can. Use it as an early filter, not as your final check — the notebook above is what actually tells you whether a question works.

6

How your questions are graded

After the deadline, every student's 6 questions are sent to the model, one at a time, using the exact same setup for everyone:

  • The model sees only a fixed system prompt, your question text, and one fixed instruction asking it to give a final numeric answer
  • The model gets exactly one attempt — no retries, no extra hints, no tools
  • A question counts as a stump if the model finishes and its final answer does not match your declared answer within your declared tolerance. If the model finishes but its final line contains no readable number, that also counts as a stump, and a TA checks the response by hand
  • If the model runs out of its 8192-token budget before finishing — usually because it fell into a repetition loop — the run produces no result and the question does not count as a stump. The notebook shows you when this happens, so you can rewrite the question before you submit

Separately, a teaching assistant checks that your written solution is actually correct and that the question really belongs to the topic you chose. A question with a wrong or unclear solution does not earn stump marks, even if the model also happened to get it wrong — the point is to find genuine weaknesses, not to submit broken questions.

How marks are decided for each part

Valid questions and solutions (54 marks, 9 per question):

  • Full marks: self-contained question, on-topic, one clear number as the answer, and a complete, correct solution
  • Small deductions: right answer, mostly right solution, but a step is unclear or the tolerance you set is a bit too loose or too tight
  • Partial marks: your solution has a real mistake but shows you understood the right method, or the question is borderline not really "computational"
  • Little or no marks: your stated answer doesn't match your own solution, the topic is wrong, the question needs a figure or outside data to solve, or it's unclear what single number is being asked for

A question with little or no marks here also cannot earn stump marks — a broken question "beating" the model doesn't count.

Stumps (32 marks, 8 each, capped at 4): this part is decided automatically by running your questions through the model, as described in Section 6. Only questions that pass the check above are eligible.

Failure reasons (14 marks total):

  • High marks: names a specific reason tied to exactly this question, not a generic guess
  • Middle marks: plausible, but generic — could be copy-pasted onto almost any question unchanged
  • Low marks: boilerplate text, or doesn't match the reason category you picked

A good reason still earns full marks even if that particular question does not end up stumping the model — you are marked on the quality of your thinking, not on the outcome.

Example: 6 valid, well-written questions gives the full 54; 2 real stumps gives 2 × 8 = 16. That's 70 from those two parts alone, plus up to 14 more depending on how specific your failure-reason writing is — solid, honest work like this lands roughly in the low-to-mid 80s out of 100.

Bonus: beating a much stronger model

This bonus is not added to Assignment 1's own score. If one of your questions also stumps a frontier-level model — GPT-5.5 or later, or Claude 4.7 or later — you earn up to 10 percentage points added to your Assignment 2 score, capped so Assignment 2 cannot go above 100%. It only helps if your Assignment 2 mark was below full marks.

To claim it, add the optional bonus_note field to that question's line in your file (see Section 4): name the exact model and version you tested, the date, and what it answered. Keep a screenshot or a shareable link to the conversation in case a TA asks to see it.

8

Copying

We compare every student's questions against every other student's, using an automatic similarity check. Two very similar questions from different students get flagged for a TA to look at by hand. This is only a flag for review, never an automatic penalty — and we never reward a question for being "more different," so there is no reason to deliberately make your questions strange just to avoid a flag.
