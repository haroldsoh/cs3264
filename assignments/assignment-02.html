Repair the Model

Course: CS3264 Foundations of Machine Learning Individual work Deadline: Friday 13 November 2026, 23:59 (same as Assignment 1) Assignment 1: Stump the Model ↗

In one sentence: take a question that Qwen3-1.7B gets wrong, build a repair that makes it answer correctly, and test whether the repair also works on questions it has not seen.

In Assignment 1 you found questions that the model gets wrong. In this assignment you fix one of those mistakes. The fix must be reusable: it has to work on new questions, not just the one you started from. You will test it the way an ML method is tested, with a baseline, a control, held-out questions, and a check that it does not break answers that were already right.

  1. Diagnose — validate one question and find the exact step where the model goes wrong.
  2. Repair — design one method that can act on a new question.
  3. Evaluate — compare the baseline, your repair, and a control on six questions.

What you need to do

Your task is to choose one ML question that the unmodified Qwen3-1.7B gets wrong, explain the mistake it makes, and build one repair that fixes it. Then compare three things on the same questions: the original model (the baseline), the model with your repair, and a control that lets you tell what your repair actually contributes.

Write up an explanation that is supported by your experimental results. Describe what fails, why your repair might help, and how the results support your reasoning. A careful, well-supported negative result earns full marks.

Training the model is optional. Prompting, worked examples, retrieval, tools, verification, and parameter-efficient finetuning are all acceptable repairs.

You submit one ZIP file through Canvas. It contains a short PDF report, your notebook with saved results, a JSON file of your experiment records, and a brief README (Section 7). Start small: read the notebook examples and test your candidate question first. You do not need all six evaluation questions before you begin.

Choose and validate your question

Start from one of your A1 questions, or write a new one. The question must be within the course scope, have one unambiguous numeric answer, and come with your own derivation. Use the A1 topic names: regression_prob, classification, pac_learning, nn_backprop, kernels_svm, and unsupervised.

The two examples in the notebook are for illustration only. Do not submit them, or numerical or wording variants of them, as your own questions, and do not use their recorded responses as evidence. You may work on the same topics and adapt the repair ideas.

Then, in this order:

  1. Solve the question and check the answer a second way, with a second derivation or a numerical check. An unchecked answer from another LLM does not count as a check.
  2. Run the unmodified baseline (defined in Section 6) and read its complete response. Find the exact step where it goes wrong.
  3. Keep the complete response as evidence. You will run this question again in the final evaluation (Section 5).
  4. Write related variants of the question and say what each one changes: the numbers, the wording, the representation, or a mathematical assumption.

An ambiguous question, an unfinished response, or a parsing failure is not a mathematical mistake. Fix an invalid question before you test any repair, and say so if you changed your A1 wording. Report what the baseline did, including the cases where it got the answer right.

A1 and A2 use the same model and the same inference settings. Even so, check the actual baseline response. If the model answers your A1 question correctly here, choose another question.

4

Build your repair

Choose one main method, or a combination you can justify. Some options:

  • a reusable prompt that makes the model check a condition or reason in a particular way
  • worked examples, correction notes, or retrieval from a fixed reference collection
  • numerical or symbolic tools, with their role stated clearly
  • a verifier, a selector, or a multi-call procedure
  • LoRA or another parameter-efficient finetuning method
  • any other inference-time method, test-time scaling strategy (such as generating several candidates and selecting one), or training approach, as long as it is built on Qwen3-1.7B

This list is not exhaustive.

What your repair may use. While developing it, you may use the anchor question, the development variant, and their solutions, for training or as fixed examples. Keep the held-out and regression questions and their solutions out of training data, example collections, reference material, and tuning. Scores on the anchor and the development variant measure performance on material the repair has seen. Scores on the held-out questions measure transfer.

What your repair must do. It must run on a new question with no help from you. It may pick a rule or a reference based on the question text. It must not receive the question's answer, grading feedback, or hints you wrote for that question. Returning stored answers, or looking up question IDs, is not a repair.

Keep the target model fixed. The repair may train the weights of Qwen3-1.7B, but the baseline you compare against stays unmodified. Do not switch to a stronger model for evaluation. If a tool computes the final answer, record the raw model response and the final system response separately. Cite any references and data your method uses.

Include one control or ablation. A control tells you what your repair contributes. For example, compare a targeted mathematical reminder with a generic "check your work" prompt, or compare a verifier with a method that uses the same number of calls. An ablation removes one component of your repair, such as the reference card. If your card both gives formulas and asks for concise reasoning, a single run cannot tell you which part helped. Choose one comparison that suits your method. You do not need to try all of these.

5

Freeze and evaluate

You evaluate on six questions. Each group answers a different question about your repair:

GroupNumberPurpose
Original anchor1The baseline gets this wrong. It establishes the mistake you are fixing.
Development variant1Use it to improve your repair before the final test.
Held-out variants2Test whether the frozen repair works beyond the questions you developed it on. One numerical/wording variant and one condition/structural variant of your anchor.
Regression checks2Test that the repair does not break answers the baseline already gets right.

Why these groups? A repair that fixes only the original question may just be tailored to that one example. The development variant lets you improve the method. The held-out variants test transfer. The regression checks reveal side effects. Six questions is a small experiment, and your conclusions should say so. You write the three variants and pick the two regression checks. The anchor can be your A1 question.

Variants. A numerical/wording variant keeps the mathematics the same and changes the numbers or the wording. A condition/structural variant changes an assumption or a relationship so that the solution has to be reworked. For example, replace a linear activation with a named nonlinear one in a gradient question. Say which anchor each variant comes from and solve every variant yourself. It does not matter if the baseline gets a held-out variant right. Evaluate all six questions anyway.

Regression checks are valid ML questions outside your failure case that the unmodified model answers correctly. A regression is a previously correct answer that becomes wrong. For example, a likelihood hint might fix a probability question but break a gradient calculation that was right before. The notebook supplies four candidate questions, and you may also write your own. Verify their solutions, then run the baseline on them and pick two that it gets right. Do this before you test the repair on them. A candidate that the baseline gets wrong cannot be a regression check. Do not use the two regression checks while you develop your repair. Once you have chosen them, keep them, even if your repair gets them wrong.

Freeze before you test. Write and verify all six questions and solutions before you run the final evaluation. "Held-out" means that you never use those questions' model outputs to choose or adjust your repair. Freeze your prompt, code, reference collection, adapter, and settings, then evaluate all six questions. If you change the method after seeing the held-out results, keep the original records, label that run as development, and write fresh held-out questions with new IDs for the final test.

Calls. Run the baseline, your repair, and the control on all six questions. For single-call methods that is 18 calls. Then repeat the baseline and the repair on the anchor in a fresh session, which adds two. Development and screening need more. You may use at most 100 calls to the target model in total. This includes retries, optional reruns of the examples, and every call inside a multi-call method. Reading the recorded examples uses none. Each call may generate at most 8192 tokens. If you train, report the training compute separately and keep to the same evaluation budget.

Reserve at least 20 calls for the final comparison and the anchor repeats. If only your repair changes during development, rerun only the repair and keep the earlier baseline and control results. Notebook Section 5 shows a 31-call plan, with two calls per extra repair-only iteration. Count interrupted calls and any calls made outside the runner, and record them in the notebook.

For a fresh repeat, restart the kernel, reload the frozen method, and use a new run name. Reusing a run name resumes its saved results instead of running again. Before you leave a temporary runtime, download the log backup described in the notebook, and restore it in the next session before you continue. The frozen final run is your main comparison. Report development trials and repeats separately.

Scoring. The notebook's scorer accepts an answer if it is within 1e-5 absolute or 1% relative of yours, whichever is larger. Keep the four outcomes separate: correct, incorrect, unparsed, and truncated. Look at any extraction that seems off, and read the reasoning. A response may give a clear boxed answer without the required prefix. Keep its automatic status, record your own review separately, and apply the same rule to every method. Never take a provisional number from unfinished thinking as the answer.

The extractor reads the same formats as the A1 extractor: Markdown bold, a Unicode minus sign, x = 0.2, trailing units, decimals, fractions, scientific notation, and exponentials such as e^{-2}. It ignores a percent sign, so 20% is read as the number 20. It does not read radicals such as sqrt(2), so review those by hand. Give your answers enough decimal places for the tolerance. Do not change the baseline's instruction.

What to report. For each group, give accuracy as a fraction (correct out of total), the wrong-to-right and right-to-wrong changes, how many responses completed and how many parsed, and the calls, tokens, time, and hardware used. Decide which run is your final run before you look at its results, and report the anchor repeats separately. Do not pick the best run afterwards.

Automatic accuracy for a method is the number of its six final responses scored correct, divided by 6. Completed-response mathematical accuracy counts only responses that finished (not truncated), including unparsed ones that you reviewed, and uses that number as the denominator. Report it next to the automatic accuracy, not instead of it. If no response finished, report N/A. For a single group, divide by that group's size instead of 6. A response that hit the token limit counts as not delivered. It is not evidence of a mathematical mistake.

These metrics score the delivered response. For ordinary prompting that is the raw model response. If a tool or a selector changes the delivered answer, use the template's final_system_response, system_predicted, system_status, and system_truncated fields for the main comparison, keep both texts, apply the same scorer to both, and report the raw model's scores separately.

A long response can take several minutes, depending on your GPU and the length of the response. The runner announces each new call. If your method changes the decoding settings, record the changes in the method_runtime_overrides field described in the JSON template.
