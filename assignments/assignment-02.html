1. What you need to do

In Assignment 1, you identified questions that expose mistakes in a fixed language model. In Assignment 2, design a reusable repair and test whether it helps on new questions.

Choose one valid ML question, explain the observed mistake, and develop one primary repair. Compare the original model, your repair, and a meaningful control. Your contribution is a mathematical explanation supported by controlled experiments: what fails, why your intervention might help, and what the results actually show.

Training is optional. Prompting, examples, retrieval, tools, verification, and parameter-efficient finetuning are all possible approaches. A careful, well-supported negative result can earn full marks.

Your task at a glance: one original failure, one reusable repair, one control, and a six-question evaluation. Submit one ZIP through Canvas containing a short PDF report, a notebook with saved results, a structured JSON file, and a brief README, plus any necessary method files. Start by reading the notebook examples and testing just your candidate anchor; you do not need to prepare all six questions before beginning.

2. Choose and validate your questions

Start from one of your A1 questions, or write a new question of your own. It must be within the course scope, have one unambiguous numeric answer, and include your own derivation.

The two notebook examples are for illustration only. Do not submit them or their numerical or wording variants as any of your own questions, or use their recorded results as your experiment evidence. You may investigate the same ML topics and adapt the repair ideas.

  1. Solve the question and independently check the answer. Use a second derivation or a numerical checker; an unchecked answer from another LLM is not an answer key.
  2. Run the unmodified Qwen3-1.7B baseline, defined in Section 6, and inspect its complete response. Identify the exact incorrect step.
  3. Keep the complete response as evidence. Repeat the selected anchor as part of the final evaluation in Section 5.
  4. Make related variants and explain what changes: numbers, wording, representation, or a mathematical assumption.

An ambiguous question, unfinished generation, or parsing failure is not evidence of a mathematical mistake. Fix invalid questions before testing repairs, and disclose changes to your A1 wording. Report baseline outcomes honestly; do not selectively report failures.

A1 and A2 use the same base model and live inference settings. Inspect your actual baseline response to confirm a mathematical mistake; if it answers correctly, choose another valid question. A failure against a different model, or an unreadable answer, is not sufficient evidence.

Use the A1 topic blocks: regression_prob, classification, pac_learning, nn_backprop, kernels_svm, and unsupervised. Model explanations are observable evidence, not proof of the model's internal mechanism.

3. Example: from failure to repair

The notebook contains two complete questions, checked solutions, and actual Qwen3-1.7B responses. The recorded baseline and repair runs use the A1-aligned configuration in Section 6 on an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4060. Responses can differ across runtimes; inspect the results from your own setup.

Example Original answer Correct answer Observable mistake
Gaussian mean with unequal known variances 3 57/31 ≈ 1.83871 Uses the ordinary mean instead of weighting by inverse variance.
Bernoulli naive Bayes with observed zero features 0.2727 9/65 ≈ 0.13846 Fails to complement the probability of the observed zero X3=0 in class 0.

The example repair appends a general likelihood reference card to each question. The same Qwen3-1.7B then generates a new response with unchanged weights. The card contains formulas, not the answers to the example questions. Full question texts, solutions, and complete original and repaired responses appear directly in the notebook.

In the recorded demonstration, the reference card repairs the Gaussian answer (57/31, scored correct) but the Bernoulli answer remains wrong (0.6, scored incorrect). The Gaussian response uses a boxed fraction after Final Answer:, which the extractor accepts. Report unsuccessful repairs as well as successes; a correct reference formula does not guarantee that the model applies it correctly.

These examples show the workflow; they are not submission questions. Develop your own investigation, use a control, and evaluate variants of your own anchor.

4. Build your open repair

Choose one primary method or a justified combination. The following suggestions are not an exhaustive list:

  • A reusable reasoning or condition-checking prompt.
  • Worked examples, correction notes, or retrieval from a frozen reference collection.
  • Numerical or symbolic tools, with their role made explicit.
  • A verifier, selector, or multi-call procedure.
  • LoRA or another parameter-efficient finetuning method.
  • Any other justified inference-time method, test-time scaling strategy (such as generating and selecting among candidates), or training approach based on Qwen3-1.7B.

During development, you may use anchor and development questions and their solutions for training or fixed examples. Keep held-out and regression questions and solutions out of training, example/reference collections, and repair tuning. Final scores on anchors and development variants measure performance on seen material; held-out results test transfer.

Your repair must run automatically on a new question. It may select a rule or reference from the question text, but must not receive that new question's answer key, grading feedback, or manually written hints. Directly returning stored answers or looking up question IDs is not a repair.

Keep Qwen3-1.7B as the target model. Its weights may be trained for your repair, but the comparison baseline must remain unmodified. Do not replace it with a stronger model at evaluation. If a tool computes the final answer, distinguish the raw model response from the final system response. Cite the references and data your method uses.

Include one meaningful control or ablation. A control helps explain what your repair contributes: compare a targeted mathematical reminder with generic checking, or a verifier with a method using the same call budget. An ablation removes a component, such as the reference card. A card that adds both formulas and a request for concise reasoning does not isolate either effect on its own. Choose one suitable comparison; you do not need to implement every suggestion.

5. Freeze and evaluate

Use a small 6-question evaluation set. Each group answers a different question about your repair:

Group Number Purpose
Original anchor 1 The baseline gets this wrong. Establish the mistake you want to fix.
Development variant 1 Try and improve your repair before the final test.
Held-out variants 2 Check whether the frozen repair works beyond the questions used to develop it. Include one numerical/wording variant and one condition/structural variant of your anchor.
Regression checks 2 Check that the repair preserves answers the baseline already gets right.

Why these groups? Fixing only the original question could mean tailoring a prompt to that exact example. The development variant lets you improve the method, held-out variants test transfer, and regression checks reveal side effects. This is a small teaching experiment, not a comprehensive benchmark. Reuse your A1 anchor or write your own; you only need to author three related variants and select two regression checks.

A numerical/wording variant preserves the mathematical setup. A condition/structural variant changes an assumption or relationship so that the solution must be reconsidered, such as replacing a linear activation with a specified nonlinear activation in a gradient question. State which anchor each variant comes from and independently solve every variant. Held-out variants need not all defeat the baseline; evaluate the fixed set regardless of its results.

Regression checks are valid ML questions outside your target failure cases that the unmodified Qwen3-1.7B answers correctly. Here, a regression means a previously correct answer becomes wrong. For example, a likelihood hint might fix a probability question but interfere with a previously correct gradient calculation. The notebook supplies four candidate questions, or you may provide your own. Verify their solutions and select two using baseline results only, before evaluating the repair on them. A candidate that the baseline gets wrong is not a regression check. Keep the selected checks out of repair tuning, and do not replace a check because your repair fails it.

Write and verify the questions and solutions before testing. Held-out means their model outputs are not used to choose or adjust your repair. Freeze your prompt, code, reference collection, adapter, and settings before running these questions, then evaluate all of them. If you revise the method after inspecting those results, keep the original records, label that run as development evidence, and use fresh questions with new IDs for the final held-out test. Self-authored cases are held out from tuning; they are not an independent benchmark.

Run the baseline, primary repair, and control on all 6 questions. For single-call methods the main comparison takes 18 calls. Repeat baseline and repair on the anchor in a fresh execution, adding two calls. Development trials and candidate screening may require additional calls. Use at most 100 target-model calls overall, including retries, optional live example reruns, and all calls inside multi-call methods; reading recorded examples uses no calls. This is a ceiling, not a target. Each call allows at most 8192 output tokens. For trained methods, report training compute separately and use the same evaluation budget.

Reserve at least 20 calls for the single-call final comparison and anchor repeats before spending the rest on screening and development; multi-call methods require more. If only your repair changes during development, rerun only that method and retain the earlier baseline/control evidence. Notebook Section 5 gives a 31-call example budget and a repair-only iteration that adds two calls. This example budget is not a required minimum or a guarantee. The notebook shows saved calls across runs. Count interrupted calls without a saved response and calls made outside the runner too, and record those separately in the notebook.

For a fresh repeat, restart the Python kernel, reload the frozen method, and use a new run name. Reusing a previous run name resumes its saved results and does not count as a new run. Before leaving a temporary runtime, download the log backup described in the notebook; restore it in a fresh session before continuing your experiments. Use the frozen final run for the main comparison; report development trials and repeated checks separately.

Use the notebook's numeric scorer: tolerance is the larger of 1e-5 absolute and 1% relative. Keep incorrect, unparsed, truncated, and correct outcomes separate. Inspect suspicious extractions and reasoning steps. A clear final boxed answer may be mathematically correct despite a missing prefix; preserve the automatic result and document any review, applying the same rules to every method. Do not extract provisional answers from unfinished thinking.

The extractor accepts the common A1 answer formats, including Markdown bold, Unicode minus, x = 0.2, trailing units, decimals, fractions, scientific notation, and numeric exponentials. A percent sign is ignored (20% is the number 20). Radicals such as sqrt(2) are not parsed; review a clear completed answer separately. Keep enough decimal precision for the tolerance, and keep the baseline's input instruction unchanged.

Report accuracy with numerators and denominators for each split, wrong-to-right and right-to-wrong transitions, completion and parsing rates, calls, tokens, time, and hardware. Include every assigned question in overall accuracy; also report completed-response mathematical accuracy with its denominator. An unfinished response is unsuccessful delivery, but does not establish a mathematical error. Choose the primary final_run before evaluation, and report the required anchor repeats separately. Do not select the best run after seeing results.

For each method's final run, automatic accuracy is the number scored correct divided by 6. Completed-response mathematical accuracy uses all non-truncated responses as its denominator, including completed unparsed responses; review these consistently to determine mathematical correctness. Keep this reviewed result separate from the automatic score. If no response completes, report N/A for this metric. For split-level automatic accuracy, replace 6 with that split's question count; the mathematical metric uses only completed responses in that split.

These metrics evaluate the delivered response. For ordinary prompting, that is the raw model response. If a tool or selector changes it, use the template's final_system_response, system_predicted, system_status, and system_truncated fields for the main comparison, and report the raw model scores separately. Preserve both texts and apply the same scorer. A correct final number does not establish that every reasoning step is correct.
