Lecture 10: Unsupervised Learning II — Variational Inference and Variational Autoencoders

Summary of Key Ideas

In Lecture 9, we learnt representations by reconstructing observations. An autoencoder maps an input to a code, then maps the code back to a reconstruction. We could also choose codes ourselves and inspect the decoder's outputs. But the reconstruction task did not tell us how to choose codes to generate new observations.

In this lecture, we introduce a probability model for generating observations. A prior specifies a distribution over codes. A likelihood specifies a distribution over observations for a given code. To generate an observation, we sample a code from the prior, then sample an observation from the likelihood. The posterior is the distribution of codes given an observed input.

We begin with a simpler inference problem: an energy sensor gives a noisy reading of a moving cart's squared velocity. Velocities near +1 and -1 both give squared velocities near 1. Bayes' rule gives a posterior with two peaks, but calculating its normalising constant requires an integral.

Variational inference approximates a difficult posterior with a distribution from a simpler family. We choose its parameters by optimisation. The objective is the evidence lower bound, or ELBO. For a fixed model, maximising the ELBO is equivalent to minimising reverse KL divergence to the posterior, without calculating the normalising constant.

We then apply the same idea to images. A neural decoder describes how a latent code produces an image. We compare three choices for the approximate posterior: fixing it to the prior, fitting a separate Gaussian for each image, and using an encoder to predict that Gaussian's parameters. The last choice gives a variational autoencoder (VAE). The reparameterisation trick lets the reconstruction loss train the encoder through sampled codes.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

  1. Define posterior inference and explain why the normalising integral can be difficult to calculate.
  2. Compute a KL divergence and express it as an expectation.
  3. Explain how forward and reverse KL differ, and why reverse KL is useful for variational inference.
  4. Derive the ELBO from reverse KL and explain the gap between the ELBO and the log evidence.
  5. Write the ELBO for the energy-sensor model and for a Gaussian image model.
  6. Compare fixing the approximate posterior to the prior, fitting one Gaussian per image, and predicting its parameters with an encoder.
  7. Explain what a VAE's encoder outputs and how reparameterisation allows us to train it.
  8. Distinguish reconstructing an observed image from generating a new image.

From Reconstruction to a Generative Model

Choosing Codes for Generation

Recall the autoencoder from Lecture 9:

\[\mathbf{z}=f_{\phi}(\mathbf{x}), \qquad \hat{\mathbf{x}}=g_{\theta}(\mathbf{z}).\]

The encoder f_{\phi} computes a code from an observation. The decoder g_{\theta} reconstructs the observation from that code. We trained both functions by minimising reconstruction error.

To generate a new image, we need a code to pass to the decoder. The reconstruction objective alone does not specify a probability distribution over codes. A grid of decoder outputs shows the images at selected codes, but gives no rule for sampling codes.

A latent-variable model includes variables that we do not observe directly. For images, we use a vector-valued code \mathbf{z} as the latent variable.

We specify two distributions:

\[p(\mathbf{z}), \qquad p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z}).\]

The prior p(\mathbf{z}) describes the codes before seeing an image. The likelihood p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z}) describes observations given a code. Together they define the joint distribution:

\[p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}) =p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z})p(\mathbf{z}).\]

In generation, we sample a code and then an observation. In inference, we calculate the distribution of the code given an observed input. We will first study inference for one unknown number.

Posterior Inference

An Energy Sensor

Consider a cart whose velocity is z. Its kinetic energy is \tfrac12 mz^2. For a mass of m=2, this is z^2. A sensor measures the energy with some noise:

\[x=z^2+\epsilon, \qquad \epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\tau^2), \qquad \tau=0.2.\]

Here x is the observed sensor reading. The noise standard deviation \tau is known. We also specify a prior over velocity:

\[z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1).\]

Equivalently, the model is

\[\boxed{ p(z)=\mathcal{N}(z;0,1), \qquad p(x\mid z)=\mathcal{N}(x;z^2,\tau^2). }\]

The notation \mathcal{N}(x;m,v) means the Gaussian density evaluated at x, with mean m and variance v.

Suppose the reading is x=1. Both z=+1 and z=-1 give z^2=1, so the reading does not reveal the direction of motion. Because the sensor is noisy, nearby velocities can also give readings near 1.

We calculate the posterior distribution p(z\mid x), the distribution of velocity z given the reading x.

Optional exploration: use the energy-sensor demo to change the velocity. Compare the readings for equal speeds in opposite directions.

Bayes' Rule

Bayes' rule gives

\[\boxed{ p(z\mid x)=\frac{p(x\mid z)p(z)}{p(x)}. }\]

The likelihood is larger when the squared velocity is close to the reading. The prior density is larger near zero. The denominator normalises their product so that the posterior integrates to 1.

For example, compare z=1 with z=0 when x=1:

\[p(1\mid z=1)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\tau^2}}, \qquad p(1\mid z=0)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\tau^2}} \exp\left(-\frac{1}{2\tau^2}\right).\]

With \tau=0.2, their ratio is \exp(12.5), approximately 268{,}337. The likelihood at z=1 is much larger than at z=0. It has the same value at z=-1 as at z=1.

Multiplying by the prior gives

\[p(z\mid x) \propto \exp\left( -\frac{z^2}{2} -\frac{(x-z^2)^2}{2\tau^2} \right).\]

The symbol \propto means equality up to a factor that does not depend on z. For x=1, this posterior has two peaks. The prior shifts them slightly towards zero. It is not a Gaussian distribution.

The Normalising Integral

The denominator is the evidence, also called the marginal likelihood:

\[p(x)=\int p(x\mid z)p(z)\,dz.\]

We integrate over all values of z to calculate p(x). For a continuous observation, p(x) is a density value, rather than the probability of observing exactly that real number.

We can evaluate p(x\mid z)p(z) at a selected z. Calculating its integral is a different task. For our one-dimensional example, a sufficiently fine grid gives a useful numerical approximation:

\[p(x)\approx\sum_j p(x\mid z_j)p(z_j)\,\Delta z.\]

This becomes expensive when the latent variable has many coordinates. A grid with 100 values along each of d coordinates has 100^d points. A neural likelihood can also make the integral difficult to solve analytically.

Another possibility is to sample from the prior:

\[p(x)=\mathbb{E}_{p(z)}[p(x\mid z)] \approx\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}p(x\mid z^{(k)}), \qquad z^{(k)}\sim p(z).\]

Here K is the number of samples. If the likelihood is very small at most prior samples, only a few terms contribute much to the estimate. We may need many samples to reach regions where the likelihood is large. This problem can become severe in high dimensions.

Variational inference takes another approach: approximate the posterior directly with a distribution that is easier to use.

Approximating a Distribution

A Family and an Objective

We choose an approximate posterior q from a family of distributions \mathcal{Q}. For example, let

\[q_{\mu,s}(z)=\mathcal{N}(z;\mu,s^2), \qquad \mu\in\mathbb{R},\quad s>0.\]

Each pair (\mu,s) gives a different Gaussian. The mean \mu changes its location. The standard deviation s changes its spread. The observation x and noise level \tau stay fixed while we adjust these parameters.

The approximation problem becomes an optimisation problem:

\[q^*=\arg\min_{q\in\mathcal{Q}}d(q,p),\]

where p is the target distribution and d measures disagreement. We must choose both the family and the objective. A single Gaussian cannot reproduce two separated peaks exactly, whatever objective we choose.

KL Divergence

The Kullback–Leibler divergence, or KL divergence, is

\[\boxed{ D_{\mathrm{KL}}[q\|p] =\int q(z)\log\frac{q(z)}{p(z)}\,dz. }\]

For discrete distributions, replace the integral with a sum:

\[D_{\mathrm{KL}}[q\|p] =\sum_k q_k\log\frac{q_k}{p_k}.\]

We use natural logarithms, so the units are nats. KL divergence is nonnegative. It is zero when the distributions agree, apart from sets of measure zero. However, it is generally asymmetric:

\[D_{\mathrm{KL}}[q\|p]\ne D_{\mathrm{KL}}[p\|q].\]

It is therefore not a distance in the usual mathematical sense. If q places probability where p assigns zero probability, D_{\mathrm{KL}}[q\|p] can be infinite. Terms with zero weight in a discrete sum contribute zero under the usual limiting convention.

An Expectation Is a Weighted Average

For a function h(z),

\[\mathbb{E}_q[h(z)]=\int q(z)h(z)\,dz.\]

The distribution in the subscript tells us how to weight the values being averaged. Applying this definition to KL gives

\[\begin{aligned} D_{\mathrm{KL}}[q\|p] &=\mathbb{E}_q\left[\log\frac{q(z)}{p(z)}\right]\\ &=\mathbb{E}_q[\log q(z)-\log p(z)]\\ &=\mathbb{E}_q[\log q(z)]-\mathbb{E}_q[\log p(z)]. \end{aligned}\]

If we can sample from q, an average of h(z) over those samples can estimate its expectation. We will use this when the decoder is a neural network.

A Numerical Example

Consider two distributions over outcomes 1 and 0:

Outcome Target p_k Approximation q_k \log(q_k/p_k) q_k\log(q_k/p_k)
1 0.8 0.5 -0.4700 -0.2350
0 0.2 0.5 0.9163 0.4581

Thus

\[D_{\mathrm{KL}}[q\|p] =0.5\log\frac{0.5}{0.8} +0.5\log\frac{0.5}{0.2} \approx0.2231.\]

An individual contribution can be negative. Nonnegativity applies to the full sum.

Swapping the distributions changes both the ratios and their weights:

\[D_{\mathrm{KL}}[p\|q] =0.8\log\frac{0.8}{0.5} +0.2\log\frac{0.2}{0.5} \approx0.1927.\]

The two values differ. If we set q=p, every nonzero term has a log ratio of zero and both divergences are zero.

Forward and Reverse KL

Relative to a target distribution p, we call

\[D_{\mathrm{KL}}[p\|q] =\mathbb{E}_p[\log p-\log q]\]

the forward KL, and

\[D_{\mathrm{KL}}[q\|p] =\mathbb{E}_q[\log q-\log p]\]

the reverse KL, also called backward KL.

If the target is Gaussian and our family contains that Gaussian, both objectives can reach zero by matching it. For example, a target \mathcal{N}(1,0.5^2) is matched by \mu=1 and s=0.5.

Our energy-sensor posterior is different. A single Gaussian cannot match its two peaks. The direction of KL now affects the best approximation.

Reverse KL gives a narrow Gaussian near one posterior peak. The dashed curve is an equally good alternative fit at the other peak. These are two separate approximations. Forward KL gives a broad Gaussian covering both peaks. The target posterior is the same in both panels.

Forward KL averages under the posterior. Both peaks receive substantial weight, so assigning very little q density to either peak is costly. Its best Gaussian is centred at zero, with standard deviation approximately 0.979.

Reverse KL averages under q. A broad q puts probability in the valley between the peaks, where the posterior density is low and the log ratio is large. A narrow Gaussian near either peak puts less probability in the valley and has a lower reverse KL. Here the two equally good fits have means approximately \pm0.974 and standard deviation 0.103.

These are consequences of this target and this restricted family. Reverse KL does not always select one peak. Neither objective can make a single Gaussian represent two separated peaks faithfully.

Why Use Reverse KL for Inference?

Our target is p(z\mid x). Forward KL requires an expectation under that posterior, which is the distribution we are trying to approximate.

Reverse KL averages under q, which we choose to be easy to evaluate and sample from. We can therefore estimate the expectation using samples from q. The objective still contains the posterior density:

\[\min_{\mu\in\mathbb{R},\,s>0} D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big[q_{\mu,s}(z)\|p(z\mid x)\big].\]

The next derivation removes the unknown normaliser from the optimisation objective.

The Evidence Lower Bound

Expanding the Posterior

Start from the definition of reverse KL:

\[D_{\mathrm{KL}}[q\|p(z\mid x)] =\mathbb{E}_q[\log q(z)-\log p(z\mid x)].\]

Bayes' rule gives

\[\log p(z\mid x)=\log p(x,z)-\log p(x).\]

Substituting,

\[\begin{aligned} D_{\mathrm{KL}}[q\|p(z\mid x)] &=\mathbb{E}_q[\log q(z)-\log p(x,z)+\log p(x)]\\ &=\mathbb{E}_q[\log q(z)-\log p(x,z)]+\log p(x). \end{aligned}\]

The second step moves \log p(x) outside the expectation. It does not depend on the variable z being averaged.

Now factor the joint as p(x,z)=p(x\mid z)p(z):

\[\begin{aligned} D_{\mathrm{KL}}[q\|p(z\mid x)] &=\mathbb{E}_q[\log q(z)-\log p(x\mid z)-\log p(z)] +\log p(x)\\ &=-\mathbb{E}_q[\log p(x\mid z)] +D_{\mathrm{KL}}[q(z)\|p(z)] +\log p(x). \end{aligned}\]

Define the evidence lower bound:

\[\boxed{ \mathcal{L}(q;x) =\mathbb{E}_q[\log p(x\mid z)] -D_{\mathrm{KL}}[q(z)\|p(z)]. }\]

Equivalently,

\[\mathcal{L}(q;x)=\mathbb{E}_q[\log p(x,z)-\log q(z)].\]

We can evaluate the functions inside these expectations without knowing p(x). Depending on the model and q, we may compute the expectations analytically or estimate them by sampling.

Why Maximising the ELBO Fits the Posterior

Rearranging the derivation gives

\[\boxed{ \log p(x) =\mathcal{L}(q;x) +D_{\mathrm{KL}}[q(z)\|p(z\mid x)]. }\]

For a fixed observation and model, \log p(x) is constant with respect to q. Therefore

\[\arg\max_{q\in\mathcal{Q}}\mathcal{L}(q;x) =\arg\min_{q\in\mathcal{Q}} D_{\mathrm{KL}}[q(z)\|p(z\mid x)].\]

We can optimise q using the ELBO even though we do not know the constant \log p(x).

Since KL divergence is nonnegative,

\[\mathcal{L}(q;x)\le\log p(x).\]

This explains the name: the ELBO is a lower bound on the log evidence. The gap is exactly the reverse KL to the posterior. If our family can represent the posterior and we find it, the gap is zero. A restricted family can leave a positive gap even after successful optimisation.

Two Different KL Divergences

The derivation contains two KL terms with different roles:

KL divergence Role
D_{\mathrm{KL}}[q(z)|p(z\mid x)] Measures inference error; equals the gap between log evidence and ELBO
D_{\mathrm{KL}}[q(z)|p(z)] Compares q with the prior; is a term we calculate inside the ELBO

We minimise the posterior KL indirectly by maximising the whole ELBO. We do not minimise the prior KL alone. Doing that would give q=p(z), regardless of the observation.

The expected log likelihood is larger when samples from q give a high likelihood to the observation. The prior KL measures how much q differs from the prior. Maximising the ELBO combines these two terms to fit q to the posterior.

A Worked ELBO for the Energy Sensor

Substitute the Likelihood

Recall

\[p(z)=\mathcal{N}(0,1), \quad p(x\mid z)=\mathcal{N}(x;z^2,\tau^2), \quad q(z)=\mathcal{N}(z;\mu,s^2).\]

The log density of a scalar Gaussian is

\[\log\mathcal{N}(x;m,\tau^2) =-\frac12\log(2\pi\tau^2)-\frac{(x-m)^2}{2\tau^2}.\]

Using m=z^2,

\[\mathbb{E}_q[\log p(x\mid z)] =-\frac12\log(2\pi\tau^2) -\frac{\mathbb{E}_q[(x-z^2)^2]}{2\tau^2}.\]

Here we average over possible velocities under q. The observed reading x and noise standard deviation \tau stay fixed.

Expand the square:

\[\mathbb{E}_q[(x-z^2)^2] =x^2-2x\mathbb{E}_q[z^2]+\mathbb{E}_q[z^4].\]

For z\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu,s^2), the moments we need are

\[\mathbb{E}_q[z^2]=\mu^2+s^2, \qquad \mathbb{E}_q[z^4]=\mu^4+6\mu^2s^2+3s^4.\]

Substituting and regrouping gives

\[\begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}_q[(x-z^2)^2] &=x^2-2x(\mu^2+s^2)+\mu^4+6\mu^2s^2+3s^4\\ &=(x-\mu^2-s^2)^2+4\mu^2s^2+2s^4. \end{aligned}\]

The expected squared error is not simply (x-\mu^2)^2. The spread of the distribution also matters.

Compute the KL to the Prior

For two scalar Gaussians,

\[D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big[ \mathcal{N}(\mu_1,s_1^2)\|\mathcal{N}(\mu_2,s_2^2) \big] =\log\frac{s_2}{s_1} +\frac{s_1^2+(\mu_1-\mu_2)^2}{2s_2^2} -\frac12.\]

With the standard normal prior, this becomes

\[\boxed{ D_{\mathrm{KL}}[\mathcal{N}(\mu,s^2)\|\mathcal{N}(0,1)] =\frac12(\mu^2+s^2-1-\log s^2). }\]

We can also see where this expression comes from. The two log densities satisfy

\[\log q(z)-\log p(z) =-\log s-\frac{(z-\mu)^2}{2s^2}+\frac{z^2}{2}.\]

Taking the expectation under q, we use \mathbb{E}_q[(z-\mu)^2]=s^2 and \mathbb{E}_q[z^2]=\mu^2+s^2:

\[D_{\mathrm{KL}}[q\|p(z)] =-\log s-\frac12+\frac{\mu^2+s^2}{2}.\]

Since 2\log s=\log s^2, this is the boxed formula. It is zero at \mu=0,s=1. Moving the mean away from zero, making the distribution very narrow, or making it very broad increases this KL.

The Complete Objective

Combining the likelihood expectation and Gaussian KL gives

\[\boxed{ \begin{aligned} \mathcal{L}(\mu,s;x) ={}&-\frac12\log(2\pi\tau^2)\\ &-\frac{(x-\mu^2-s^2)^2+4\mu^2s^2+2s^4}{2\tau^2}\\ &-\frac12(\mu^2+s^2-1-\log s^2). \end{aligned} }\]

The difficult integral has disappeared from this objective. For the chosen Gaussian family, we have a formula in two adjustable parameters.

We maximise it over \mu and s>0. One way to enforce positivity is to optimise an unconstrained log standard deviation a and set s=\exp(a).

For x=1 and \tau=0.2, numerical integration gives \log p(x)\approx-1.3866. Some candidate approximations give:

Approximation q ELBO Posterior KL, \log p(x)-\mathcal{L}
\mathcal{N}(0,1) -24.3095 22.9229
\mathcal{N}(1,0.2^2) -2.9989 1.6123
Best Gaussian near the positive peak, \mathcal{N}(0.97384,0.10269^2) -2.0889 0.7023

Each row satisfies the same identity: ELBO plus posterior KL equals log evidence. Here, a less negative ELBO is better.

For a fixed model, log evidence stays unchanged as we adjust q. The vertical gap is the posterior KL. The two equally good solutions occur because velocities with opposite signs have the same squared value.

The best Gaussian still misses one posterior peak. Its remaining gap is an approximation error from the chosen family. The negative-mean solution is equally good by symmetry.

The Gaussian mean near 0.974 is also different from the location of the posterior peak, approximately 0.990. Fitting a distribution and finding the most likely single velocity are different optimisation problems.

A Recipe for Variational Inference

The procedure is:

  1. Choose a family \mathcal{Q} for the approximate posterior.
  2. Write \mathcal{L}(q;x)=\mathbb{E}_q[\log p(x\mid z)]-D_{\mathrm{KL}}[q\|p(z)].
  3. Maximise the ELBO over the parameters of q.

The family determines what approximations are possible. The model and ELBO determine how we score them. The optimiser determines which solution we find.

A Latent-Variable Model of Images

A Gaussian Prior and a Neural Decoder

We now replace the scalar velocity with a vector-valued code:

\[\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}, \qquad p(\mathbf{z})=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I}).\]

An image is a vector \mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}. For the 28\times28 greyscale digit images in this lecture, D=784. The experiment uses d=16 latent coordinates.

A CNN decoder g_{\theta} maps the code to an image-sized vector. We use that vector as the mean of a Gaussian likelihood:

\[\boxed{ p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z}) =\mathcal{N}\big( \mathbf{x};g_{\theta}(\mathbf{z}),\tau^2\mathbf{I} \big). }\]

The decoder weights are \theta. The observation noise standard deviation \tau is fixed. In the digit experiment, \tau=0.5; the earlier sensor example used 0.2.

Given the code, this likelihood treats pixel noise as independent with the same variance. The mean image can still contain spatial structure because all its pixels are computed from the code by the neural decoder.

The log likelihood is

\[\log p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z}) =-\frac{D}{2}\log(2\pi\tau^2) -\frac{\|\mathbf{x}-g_{\theta}(\mathbf{z})\|^2}{2\tau^2}.\]

For fixed \tau, maximising this quantity is equivalent to minimising squared reconstruction error when the code is given.

Learning Without Observed Codes

Our training dataset contains images,

\[\mathcal{D}=\{\mathbf{x}_n\}_{n=1}^{N},\]

but no supplied codes. Maximum likelihood would maximise

\[\sum_{n=1}^{N}\log p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_n),\]

where

\[\begin{aligned} p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}) &=\int p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z})p(\mathbf{z})\,d\mathbf{z}\\ &=\mathbb{E}_{p(\mathbf{z})}[p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z})]. \end{aligned}\]

The integral is generally difficult for a neural decoder. Sampling codes from the prior can give a noisy estimate because most sampled codes may decode to images unlike the particular target.

Notice the order of the operations: log likelihood is the log of an expected likelihood. It is generally different from the expected log likelihood:

\[\log\mathbb{E}_{p(\mathbf{z})}[p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z})] \ne \mathbb{E}_{p(\mathbf{z})}[\log p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z})].\]

Replacing one with the other changes the objective. We will optimise the ELBO, a lower bound on the log likelihood.

Learning the Model with an ELBO

For any suitable q(\mathbf{z}),

\[\mathcal{L}(\theta,q;\mathbf{x}) =\mathbb{E}_q[\log p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z})] -D_{\mathrm{KL}}[q(\mathbf{z})\|p(\mathbf{z})].\]

The identity now includes the decoder parameters:

\[\log p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}) =\mathcal{L}(\theta,q;\mathbf{x}) +D_{\mathrm{KL}}[q(\mathbf{z})\|p_{\theta}(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{x})].\]

With \theta fixed, maximising over q fits the current model's posterior. We can also optimise \theta to improve the bound across the training images.

If q equals the exact posterior, the bound is tight. If we could maintain that equality for each model, maximising the bound would be maximum likelihood. With a restricted approximate posterior, we instead optimise a lower bound. Raising it while changing \theta need not raise the exact log likelihood by the same amount, because the posterior KL gap can change too.

We will compare three choices for q using this same generative model.

Three Choices for the Approximate Posterior

The Dataset ELBO

Each image has its own latent code \mathbf{z}_n. The model assumes independent image–code pairs:

\[p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{1:N},\mathbf{z}_{1:N}) =\prod_{n=1}^{N}p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_n\mid\mathbf{z}_n)p(\mathbf{z}_n).\]

We also choose a factorised approximation,

\[q(\mathbf{z}_{1:N})=\prod_{n=1}^{N}q_n(\mathbf{z}_n).\]

Here \mathbf{x}_{1:N} means all N images, and q_n is the approximate posterior for image n. Taking logs turns the products into sums. The ELBO becomes

\[\boxed{ \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{data}} =\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left( \mathbb{E}_{q_n}[\log p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_n\mid\mathbf{z}_n)] -D_{\mathrm{KL}}[q_n\|p(\mathbf{z}_n)] \right). }\]

Each image contributes an expected log likelihood and a prior KL. We now decide how to specify the distributions q_n.

Attempt 1: Fix Every Approximation to the Prior

The simplest choice is

\[q_n(\mathbf{z}_n)=p(\mathbf{z}_n)=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I}).\]

Every prior KL is zero. The dataset ELBO reduces to

\[\mathcal{L}_1(\theta) =-\frac{1}{2\tau^2}\sum_n \mathbb{E}_{p(\mathbf{z}_n)} \left[\|\mathbf{x}_n-g_{\theta}(\mathbf{z}_n)\|^2\right]+C,\]

where C=-ND\log(2\pi\tau^2)/2 is constant with respect to the trainable parameters. Only the decoder is learnt.

This is a valid ELBO, but the sampled code does not depend on the target image. The same code distribution must help reconstruct every image. Under squared error, this encourages the decoder to output the average training image.

To see why, fix a code and write \mathbf{y}=g_{\theta}(\mathbf{z}). Its total squared error over the images is

\[\sum_n\|\mathbf{x}_n-\mathbf{y}\|^2.\]

Differentiate with respect to \mathbf{y}:

\[\nabla_{\mathbf{y}}\sum_n\|\mathbf{x}_n-\mathbf{y}\|^2 =2N\mathbf{y}-2\sum_n\mathbf{x}_n.\]

The minimiser is

\[\mathbf{y}=\bar{\mathbf{x}} =\frac1N\sum_n\mathbf{x}_n.\]

This argument applies to each code because the code is independent of the target. A decoder capable of producing the same mean image everywhere can minimise this objective by ignoring its code.

Here, every image has the same approximate posterior. The sampled codes do not depend on the input image.

Attempt 2: Fit One Gaussian per Image

Instead, assign each training image its own Gaussian:

\[q_n(\mathbf{z}_n) =\mathcal{N}\big( \mathbf{z}_n;\boldsymbol{\mu}_n, \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{s}_n^2) \big).\]

Each mean vector and standard deviation vector has d entries. The covariance is diagonal, so the latent coordinates are independent under this approximate posterior.

We optimise all these parameters along with the decoder weights:

\[\max_{\substack{\theta,\,\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1:N}\\\mathbf{s}_{1:N}>0}} \mathcal{L}_2.\]

For a diagonal Gaussian and standard normal prior, the KL is a sum of scalar Gaussian KLs:

\[\boxed{ D_{\mathrm{KL}}[q_n\|p(\mathbf{z}_n)] =\frac12\sum_{j=1}^{d} \left(\mu_{nj}^2+s_{nj}^2-1-\log s_{nj}^2\right). }\]

Substituting into the dataset ELBO gives

\[\boxed{ \begin{aligned} \mathcal{L}_2 =\sum_{n=1}^{N}\Bigg( &-\frac{1}{2\tau^2} \mathbb{E}_{q_n}\left[ \|\mathbf{x}_n-g_{\theta}(\mathbf{z}_n)\|^2 \right]\\ &-\frac12\sum_{j=1}^{d} \left(\mu_{nj}^2+s_{nj}^2-1-\log s_{nj}^2\right) \Bigg)+C. \end{aligned} }\]

Now each image has its own code distribution. The reconstruction term is larger when sampled codes give smaller reconstruction errors. The prior KL increases if the means move far from zero or the standard deviations approach zero.

Generating and Inferring with Attempt 2

We can already generate new images with this model. Sample a new code from the prior and apply the trained decoder. Generation does not require an encoder or a new input image.

For a new observed image, however, we do not have fitted Gaussian parameters. Keep the trained decoder fixed, initialise a new \boldsymbol{\mu},\mathbf{s}, and optimise that image's ELBO. This requires a new optimisation for every new input.

Attempt 2 also stores 2Nd approximate-posterior parameters for the training set. The decoder weights are additional shared model parameters.

Attempt 3: Predict the Gaussian with an Encoder

An encoder can learn to predict the approximate-posterior parameters from the input:

\[\boldsymbol{\mu}_n=\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\phi}(\mathbf{x}_n), \qquad \mathbf{s}_n=\mathbf{s}_{\phi}(\mathbf{x}_n).\]

This defines

\[\boxed{ q_{\phi}(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{x}) =\mathcal{N}\big( \mathbf{z};\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\phi}(\mathbf{x}), \operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{s}_{\phi}(\mathbf{x})^2) \big). }\]

The encoder's weights are \phi. Its outputs are distribution parameters, rather than one deterministic code. For a d-dimensional code, a diagonal Gaussian needs d means and d standard deviations.

In practice, an encoder may output log variances \boldsymbol{\ell} rather than standard deviations directly. We then set

\[s_j=\exp(\ell_j/2).\]

This ensures positive standard deviations while allowing unconstrained network outputs. A log variance is the logarithm of s_j^2, not of s_j.

The ELBO has the same form as in Attempt 2, but the means and standard deviations now come from one shared network:

\[\max_{\theta,\phi} \sum_n\left( \mathbb{E}_{q_{\phi}(\mathbf{z}_n\mid\mathbf{x}_n)} [\log p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_n\mid\mathbf{z}_n)] -D_{\mathrm{KL}}[q_{\phi}(\mathbf{z}_n\mid\mathbf{x}_n)\|p(\mathbf{z}_n)] \right).\]

This is amortised inference: one encoder predicts the approximate-posterior parameters for all images. For a new image, we run the trained encoder once instead of fitting a new Gaussian by optimisation.

The encoder, latent sampling, and probabilistic decoder together give the variational autoencoder. We still need a way to differentiate through its sampled codes.

Does the Encoder Make the Family More Flexible?

Fix the decoder and compare the best possible training-set ELBOs for Attempts 2 and 3.

Attempt 2 can choose any allowed \boldsymbol{\mu}_n,\mathbf{s}_n for every image. Any outputs produced by an encoder are one particular choice of those parameters. Attempt 3 therefore uses a family contained in Attempt 2's family on the training observations.

Consequently, with the decoder fixed and the objectives optimised fully, Attempt 2 can match or exceed Attempt 3's best ELBO. The benefit of amortisation is fast inference and shared computation. It does not enlarge the set of per-image diagonal Gaussians available on a fixed dataset.

Both approximations still factor across images:

\[q_{\phi}(\mathbf{z}_{1:N}\mid\mathbf{x}_{1:N}) =\prod_n q_{\phi}(\mathbf{z}_n\mid\mathbf{x}_n).\]

Sharing encoder weights does not make the sampled codes statistically dependent under this distribution when the images and weights are fixed.

Training a Variational Autoencoder

Why Direct Sampling Can Block Learning

The reconstruction term contains an expectation under a distribution whose parameters depend on the encoder:

\[\mathbb{E}_{q_{\phi}(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{x})} [\log p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z})].\]

Suppose we predict the Gaussian parameters and then draw a code using a sampling operation that returns a value with no recorded gradient connection to those parameters. Backpropagation can train the decoder from the reconstruction error, but the reconstruction gradient cannot reach the encoder.

The prior KL is an analytic function of the encoder outputs, so it can still train the encoder. Without the reconstruction contribution, it encourages every approximate posterior towards \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I}). We then return towards the failure of Attempt 1: sampled codes carry little information about their target images.

This is a problem with that way of computing the gradient. The expected reconstruction term does depend on the encoder parameters. We need an estimator that captures that dependence.

The Reparameterisation Trick

For one Gaussian variable, we can sample by drawing standard normal noise and transforming it:

\[\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1), \qquad z=\mu+s\epsilon.\]

The resulting z has mean \mu and variance s^2. For a diagonal Gaussian,

\[\boxed{ \boldsymbol{\epsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I}), \qquad \mathbf{z} =\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\phi}(\mathbf{x}) +\mathbf{s}_{\phi}(\mathbf{x})\odot\boldsymbol{\epsilon}. }\]

The symbol \odot means elementwise multiplication. The source of randomness, \boldsymbol{\epsilon}, has a distribution that does not depend on \phi.

After drawing the noise, we keep its value fixed during that gradient calculation. The code is then a differentiable function of the encoder outputs:

\[\frac{\partial z_j}{\partial\mu_j}=1, \qquad \frac{\partial z_j}{\partial s_j}=\epsilon_j.\]

For example, if \mu=0.5, s=0.2, and \epsilon=-0.6, then z=0.38. With this noise value fixed, increasing \mu by 0.01 increases z by 0.01. Increasing s by 0.01 decreases z by 0.006.

The code still has the required Gaussian distribution. We can now differentiate it with respect to the encoder outputs, so the reconstruction loss can train the encoder.

Estimating the Reconstruction Expectation

Reparameterisation rewrites the expectation as

\[\begin{aligned} &\mathbb{E}_{q_{\phi}(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{x})} [\log p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z})]\\ &\quad= \mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})} \left[ \log p_{\theta}\left( \mathbf{x}\mid \boldsymbol{\mu}_{\phi}(\mathbf{x}) +\mathbf{s}_{\phi}(\mathbf{x})\odot\boldsymbol{\epsilon} \right) \right]. \end{aligned}\]

We estimate this with sampled noise vectors. One latent sample per image per update is common, and is the choice used in the lecture experiment. Different samples across updates give a stochastic estimate of the expected reconstruction term.

The diagonal Gaussian prior KL is computed exactly. We do not need to estimate it using samples.

The exact expected objective is a lower bound. A particular sampled estimate can fluctuate above or below it; an individual estimate is not guaranteed to be below the log evidence.

The Loss We Minimise

Training software usually minimises a loss. For one image and one reparameterised code sample, the negative ELBO estimate is

\[\boxed{ \begin{aligned} \widehat{J}(\theta,\phi;\mathbf{x}) ={}&\frac{\|\mathbf{x}-g_{\theta}(\mathbf{z})\|^2}{2\tau^2}\\ &+\frac12\sum_{j=1}^{d} \left(\mu_j^2+s_j^2-1-\log s_j^2\right) +\frac{D}{2}\log(2\pi\tau^2). \end{aligned} }\]

The Gaussian normalising term is constant when D and \tau are fixed, so we can omit it for gradient updates. We must retain it when reporting the actual ELBO value.

The reconstruction term uses the sum of squared pixel errors. Replacing it with the average over pixels, while leaving the KL unchanged, changes the relative weights. In this Gaussian model, that relative weighting follows from the likelihood variance \tau^2.

The roles of the standard deviations also differ. The fixed \tau describes variation in the observation given a code. The encoder's s_j describes uncertainty in latent coordinate j for a particular observed image.

One Training Step

For a minibatch of images:

  1. Run the encoder to obtain means and log variances for each image.
  2. Convert log variances to standard deviations.
  3. Draw an independent standard normal noise vector for each image.
  4. Form each code using \mathbf{z}=\boldsymbol{\mu}+\mathbf{s}\odot\boldsymbol{\epsilon}.
  5. Decode the codes and compute reconstruction losses and analytic prior KLs.
  6. Average the losses across the minibatch, backpropagate, and update both networks.

The images supply their own reconstruction targets. Digit labels are not needed. We learn a model of observations and an approximate inference procedure together.

The Experiment

The lecture experiment uses 60,000 MNIST training images, with pixel values scaled to [0,1]. It uses the same CNN decoder architecture, a 16-dimensional standard normal prior, and a Gaussian likelihood with \tau=0.5 for all attempts.

The runs use matched initial decoder weights, training-image order, batch size, and number of decoder updates. They use minibatches of 128 images, Adam with learning rate 0.001, and 30,000 decoder updates. Attempt 2 takes five optimisation steps on the Gaussian parameters for each decoder update, so it uses additional computation.

What the Comparison Shows

Attempt 1 learns outputs close to the mean image because its codes do not depend on the target. The encoder with blocked reconstruction gradients behaves similarly: its prior KL is nearly zero, but its reconstructions are poor.

The VAE has a larger prior KL and much smaller reconstruction error. Its ELBO is higher because the improvement in the reconstruction term is larger than the increase in prior KL. A small prior KL alone does not mean that the reconstructions are good.

Attempt 2 and the VAE both work in this experiment. Their results do not contradict the family comparison earlier. That comparison fixes the decoder and considers fully optimised objectives on the same observations. These runs learn different decoder weights, use finite optimisation, and report test-image results.

Reconstruction and Generation

Reconstructing an Observed Image

For an observed image, run the encoder:

\[\mathbf{x} \longrightarrow (\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\phi}(\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{s}_{\phi}(\mathbf{x})) \longrightarrow \mathbf{z}\sim q_{\phi}(\mathbf{z}\mid\mathbf{x}) \longrightarrow g_{\theta}(\mathbf{z}).\]

The code distribution depends on the input. Different samples can give different reconstructions. We can also decode the mean code for a deterministic visualisation, although in a nonlinear decoder this is generally different from the average decoded image:

\[g_{\theta}(\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{z}]) \ne \mathbb{E}[g_{\theta}(\mathbf{z})].\]

Generating a New Image

For generation, there is no input image to encode. Use the prior:

\[\boxed{ \mathbf{z}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I}), \qquad \mathbf{x}\sim p_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}\mid\mathbf{z}). }\]

For our Gaussian likelihood, a full model sample is

\[\mathbf{x}=g_{\theta}(\mathbf{z})+\tau\boldsymbol{\eta}, \qquad \boldsymbol{\eta}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I}).\]

Here \boldsymbol{\eta} has D coordinates and represents pixel noise. The latent code has d coordinates.

The lecture's generated-image displays show g_{\theta}(\mathbf{z}), the conditional mean images, without this final pixel noise. This makes the decoder's learned structure easier to inspect. A mean image and a full sample from the stated likelihood are different outputs.

We sample from the prior to generate new images. For an observed image, we use its approximate posterior to sample codes for reconstruction. Once training is complete, generation only needs the decoder and prior.

The VAE specifies distributions over codes and observations. Its encoder approximates the posterior. We train the encoder and decoder together by maximising the ELBO.

Summary

Posterior inference means calculating the distribution of z given an observed x. Bayes' rule gives this distribution as likelihood times prior, divided by the evidence. Calculating the evidence can require a difficult integral.

Variational inference chooses a simpler distribution q and fits it to the posterior. Reverse KL averages under q, giving us an expectation we can work with. Expanding Bayes' rule yields

\[\log p(x) =\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_q[\log p(x\mid z)]-D_{\mathrm{KL}}[q\|p(z)]}_{\text{ELBO}} +\underbrace{D_{\mathrm{KL}}[q\|p(z\mid x)]}_{\text{posterior approximation gap}}.\]

For a fixed model, maximising the ELBO minimises the posterior KL. The prior KL inside the objective is a different quantity from that approximation gap.

A VAE combines a latent prior, a probabilistic decoder, and an encoder that predicts approximate-posterior parameters. The encoder replaces separate optimisation for each new image with a shared learned computation. Reparameterisation makes sampled codes differentiable functions of those parameters and independent noise, allowing reconstruction gradients to train the encoder.

To reconstruct an observation, infer its code using the encoder. To generate a new observation, sample its code from the prior and use the decoder. Next, we will study diffusion models as another way to model and generate observations.
