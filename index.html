What this course is

Machine learning is the study of models and algorithms for finding patterns in data. CS3264 is about the machinery underneath — the part that transfers when the tools change.

By the end you should be able to

  • Describe what ML is, especially supervised and unsupervised learning.
  • Explain fundamental techniques, how they work — and when they will not.
  • Design your own ML methods.

This course is not

  • a pure mathematics or statistics course
  • a programming course
  • a complete survey of machine learning

You will go beyond calling libraries: reading state-of-the-art methods, adapting them, and debugging models that misbehave.

Resources

Everything here is free.

Main textbook

Pattern Recognition and Machine Learning — Christopher Bishop. The weekly readings in the schedule refer to this one.

Do not overlook the appendices — they are a compact reference for most of the mathematics the chapters assume: B probability distributions, C matrix identities, D calculus of variations, E Lagrange multipliers.

Alternate textbook — and the basics

Mathematics for Machine Learning — Deisenroth, Faisal & Ong. Useful two ways.

As a second treatment when Bishop’s presentation of a topic does not land — the same material, explained differently, is often all it takes.

And for the background itself: Chapters 2–5 cover essentially all the linear algebra, vector calculus and probability this course assumes.

Past years’ Collaborative Notes

Earlier cohorts built one shared set of notes covering the whole syllabus — probability and vector calculus through to PAC bounds, kernels, EM and VAEs.

AY2025/26 notes — PDF, 37 pages. Written by students, so treat them as a study aid rather than an authority: verify anything you intend to rely on.

They may not be brought into either exam this year. The midterm is fully closed book; the final allows a two-page cheat sheet you write yourself.

Code

Lecture notebooks are on Google Colab; links appear with each lecture. They are written to be read, not just run — the implementations follow the equations as we derive them in the course, rather than the fastest library call.

Policies

Academic integrity

The principle is simple: your graded work should be your own. NUS has a strict policy on plagiarism; cases are reported to the department, and if found guilty you will fail the course.

Fine: discussing ideas and approaches; using public information in your own words, with attribution.
Not fine: copying verbatim; sharing code or write-ups.

There are always better options — ask on Piazza, arrange a consultation, or hand in something incomplete. All of those are recoverable.

On using LLMs

You may use them. We would rather set a sensible policy than pretend otherwise.

Use them as a tutor — explanations, analogies, hints when stuck. Try opening with: “You are a teaching assistant in a machine learning class. Your goal is to help me understand. Do not give me the answer directly.”

Do not paste their output into graded work. That is plagiarism.

They are good at this material now — which is the problem. A derivation you did not do teaches you nothing, and both exams are on paper. The errors that remain are subtle: a dropped factor of ½, a convention that is not ours, a bound stated almost right — the kind you can only catch if you could have done it yourself.
